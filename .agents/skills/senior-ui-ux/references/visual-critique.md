# Visual Critique

Use this reference to evaluate visual quality, art direction, and polish. Combine it with [review.md](review.md) when task flow, interaction, accessibility, or acceptance is also in scope.

## Establish the evidence

Prefer rendered evidence showing representative content, states, themes, and viewport sizes. A single polished screenshot can support composition feedback but cannot prove interaction quality, responsive behavior, accessibility, or system consistency.

Record:

- what artifact and state were reviewed;
- viewport, theme, content, and platform where known;
- the intended audience, task, brand, and visual thesis;
- established design-system constraints;
- what could not be observed.

If no visual thesis exists, infer the apparent direction cautiously and flag the absence as a source of drift rather than inventing intent.

## Review from large to small

### 1. First impression and focal path

- What receives attention first, second, and third?
- Does that order match the user's task and the intended message?
- Is the purpose understandable before reading every detail?
- Does the emotional impression fit the product and audience?

Use a brief squint or grayscale inspection when helpful to reveal competing emphasis and weak grouping. Treat it as a diagnostic, not a substitute for normal viewing.

### 2. Composition and rhythm

- Does the macro layout create a clear structure across the whole surface?
- Are alignment, balance, whitespace, density, and section rhythm intentional?
- Are there monotonous repetitions, accidental gaps, cramped transitions, or isolated elements?
- Does the composition remain coherent with realistic content and at relevant viewport sizes?

### 3. Typography

- Do type choices fit the voice, content, language, and platform?
- Are roles distinguishable without excessive styles?
- Are measure, leading, wrapping, weight, spacing, and numerals handled carefully?
- Does expressive typography add identity without harming sustained reading or operational clarity?

### 4. Color, surfaces, and depth

- Does color guide attention and communicate meaning?
- Are brand, action, and semantic colors distinct and proportioned deliberately?
- Do borders, radius, shadows, texture, translucency, and elevation form one material language?
- Are contrast and state differences measurable rather than assumed?

### 5. Imagery, icons, and data

- Are visual assets relevant to the subject rather than interchangeable decoration?
- Are crops, focal points, aspect ratios, and empty states intentional?
- Is icon style optically consistent and understandable?
- Do charts make the intended comparison visible without distorting or hiding information?

### 6. Motion and interaction character

- Does motion explain change, provide feedback, preserve continuity, or express an appropriate character?
- Are timing and easing coherent across the experience?
- Is motion concentrated at meaningful moments rather than applied uniformly?
- Does reduced motion preserve information and orientation?

### 7. Coherence, specificity, and finish

- Can the major choices be traced to one visual thesis?
- Which choices feel specific to this product, and which appear inherited from a generic template?
- Are repeated patterns consistent without becoming monotonous?
- Are edge alignment, optical centering, wrapping, state styling, and small details resolved?
- Does the direction extend credibly to routine, dense, empty, error, and mobile states?

Do not call a design generic merely because it uses a common component or font. Identify the repeated unexamined decisions and explain how they weaken product fit.

## Diagnose before prescribing

Name the underlying issue rather than listing every symptom. For example, several competing button styles may indicate missing action hierarchy; inconsistent gaps may indicate no spacing rhythm; decorative treatments may indicate an absent visual thesis.

Prioritize corrections in this order when applicable:

1. product fit and focal hierarchy;
2. macro composition and content structure;
3. typography and color systems;
4. component and state consistency;
5. imagery, motion, and signature expression;
6. optical polish.

Do not recommend surface polish as the remedy for a structural problem.

## Report

Lead with the visual verdict: what works, what holds the design back, and the highest-leverage direction. Then provide prioritized findings using the evidence, classification, impact, recommendation, and validation fields from [review.md](review.md).

For aesthetic findings, distinguish:

- a coherence or hierarchy problem that affects comprehension;
- a conflict with an agreed brand or design-system convention;
- a craft weakness that reduces perceived quality;
- a defensible preference with multiple valid answers;
- an untested hypothesis about audience response.

When proposing a refinement, say what perceptual or product outcome should change. Avoid redesigning the interface through a list of disconnected stylistic substitutions.
