# Visual Design

Use this reference to define or assess the craft of an interface. Visual quality is not a style preset. It is the intentional control of attention, meaning, rhythm, character, and detail in service of a particular product and audience.

## Begin with intent

Before choosing visual treatments, establish:

- what the interface should help the user notice, understand, feel, and do;
- what qualities the product must communicate, such as precision, warmth, authority, speed, playfulness, or calm;
- which brand assets and conventions are established;
- what content, platform, environment, and accessibility constraints shape the design;
- whether the surface is primarily operational, persuasive, expressive, exploratory, or transactional.

Do not use "modern," "clean," "premium," or "beautiful" as sufficient direction. Translate them into observable choices and contextual reasons.

## Composition and hierarchy

- Define a focal path: what should be seen first, second, and third.
- Organize the page at the macro level before decorating components. Use regions, alignment, scale, whitespace, density, and contrast to express priority.
- Choose a grid and alignment logic appropriate to the content. Break it only when the exception strengthens meaning or emphasis.
- Use asymmetry, overlap, or unconventional composition when it improves the visual thesis, not merely to appear creative.
- Control simultaneous emphasis. If every section demands attention, none has hierarchy.
- Treat whitespace and density as active design variables. Spaciousness is not automatically refined, and compactness is not automatically cluttered.
- Compose across the full viewport and journey. A sequence of individually attractive cards can still make a weak page.

## Typography

- Choose type for voice, readability, language coverage, platform constraints, and content density.
- Define roles before sizes: display, title, heading, body, label, annotation, data, and code where applicable.
- Create hierarchy using a limited combination of family, size, weight, width, spacing, case, and color.
- Check measure, line height, wrapping, numerals, punctuation, and long or translated content.
- Use expressive typography where it contributes identity; keep sustained reading and operational text quiet and legible.
- Avoid selecting or rejecting a typeface solely because it is popular. Generic use is the problem, not the font itself.

## Color and contrast

- Give each color a job: foundation, surface, text, action, status, accent, visualization, or decoration.
- Define proportional use. A palette is not a command to use every color equally.
- Build attention with contrast in value, saturation, temperature, and area, while meeting applicable contrast requirements.
- Separate brand expression from semantic status. Error, warning, success, and focus must remain understandable and consistent.
- Evaluate light, dark, high-contrast, disabled, selected, and data states where relevant.
- Do not rely on gradients, muted gray, or a bright accent to create identity without a product-specific reason.

## Spacing, shape, and surfaces

- Establish a spacing rhythm that supports grouping and reading. Optical balance may require justified exceptions to a mathematical scale.
- Use radius, border, elevation, texture, and translucency as a coherent material language.
- Vary containment according to structure. Do not turn every piece of content into an equal card.
- Make interactive boundaries and grouping clear without excessive boxes, dividers, or shadows.
- Ensure surface treatments survive different themes, contrast modes, content lengths, and viewport sizes.

## Imagery, iconography, and data

- Define the role of imagery: explanation, evidence, atmosphere, identity, or storytelling.
- Prefer subject-specific imagery or illustration direction over interchangeable stock decoration.
- Keep icon metaphor, stroke, fill, optical size, and detail level coherent. Icons must clarify rather than substitute for unfamiliar labels.
- Match data visualization to the comparison or pattern the user needs to perceive. Visual styling must not obscure scales, uncertainty, labels, or accessibility.
- Specify cropping, focal points, aspect-ratio behavior, alternatives, and empty or unavailable states.

## Motion

- Give motion a purpose: feedback, continuity, orientation, hierarchy, or character.
- Define its character using qualities such as direct, restrained, elastic, mechanical, fluid, or playful, and connect that character to the visual thesis.
- Prefer a few coordinated moments over continuous unrelated effects.
- Keep task-critical feedback prompt. Avoid motion that delays access, destabilizes layout, or competes with reading.
- Specify a reduced-motion equivalent that preserves information and state change.

## Responsive visual behavior

Responsive design may change composition, priority, density, navigation, and interaction, not just dimensions.

- Preserve the focal path at each supported width.
- Recompose relationships before shrinking important content beyond usefulness.
- Decide what wraps, stacks, scrolls, collapses, moves, summarizes, or remains fixed.
- Account for touch, pointer, keyboard, safe areas, zoom, large text, orientation, and virtual keyboards where relevant.
- Test real content at narrow, intermediate, and wide conditions rather than designing only named devices.

## Coherence and distinctiveness

A coherent interface has a small number of reinforcing ideas. A distinctive interface has choices traceable to this product rather than to a reusable aesthetic recipe.

Look for a signature in one or two suitable places: composition, type treatment, imagery, interaction, data expression, or another product-specific device. Keep supporting elements disciplined.

Common signs of an unexamined default include repetitive equal cards, decorative gradient fields, excessive pills, arbitrary glass effects, generic centered marketing compositions, mismatched icon styles, animation on every element, and interchangeable placeholder copy. These are diagnostic signals, not bans. Keep any of them when the context and visual thesis justify them.

## System quality

Define reusable rules where repetition carries meaning: semantic color, type roles, spacing, focus, states, surfaces, icons, and motion. Preserve room for page-level composition and optical judgment.

Before proposing a new token or primitive, determine whether the established system already expresses the needed meaning. A visual system should reduce arbitrary decisions without making every surface look identical.
