# Art Direction

Use this reference when establishing, comparing, or repairing the visual identity of an interface. Art direction selects the coherent idea behind the design; visual design turns that idea into a system.

## Gather the creative brief

Establish the minimum context that can materially change the direction:

- product, audience, task, and environment;
- desired perception and emotional tone;
- brand truths, assets, and non-negotiables;
- real content, subject matter, and domain vocabulary;
- competitors, category conventions, and meaningful differentiation;
- technical, accessibility, performance, localization, and delivery constraints;
- references the user admires or rejects, including what specifically they respond to.

When references are unavailable, proceed with labeled assumptions. Do not infer a brand personality from the product category alone.

## Derive rather than decorate

Build direction from material already present in the problem: language, workflows, physical or cultural references, data, audience expectations, product history, and brand behavior. Avoid attaching a fashionable style to an unrelated product.

Separate three decisions:

1. **Experience posture**: how the product behaves and communicates, such as calm guidance, rapid operation, focused authority, or playful exploration.
2. **Visual thesis**: one sentence connecting intended perception to composition, type, color, imagery, and motion.
3. **Signature device**: one or two memorable treatments that express the thesis without overwhelming the experience.

## Explore directions when uncertainty matters

When the visual direction is genuinely unresolved, propose two or three meaningfully different directions. Vary the underlying idea, not merely the accent color.

For each direction, describe:

- the visual thesis and intended emotional effect;
- why it fits the audience, product, and content;
- macro-composition and density;
- typography character and roles;
- color logic and proportional use;
- imagery, iconography, surface, and motion language;
- signature device;
- major accessibility, performance, brand, or extensibility risks;
- where the direction should deliberately remain restrained.

Recommend one direction. Do not make the user select from an unexplained mood-board menu.

## Evaluate the direction

Judge candidate directions using evidence and explicit tradeoffs:

- **Fit**: does it serve the product, audience, task, and brand?
- **Hierarchy**: does it focus attention on what matters?
- **Coherence**: do the elements reinforce the same thesis?
- **Distinctiveness**: would the interface retain recognizable character without its logo?
- **Usability**: does expression preserve comprehension and control?
- **Accessibility**: can the direction meet applicable requirements without losing its core idea?
- **Feasibility**: can it be delivered and maintained within real constraints?
- **Range**: can it support dense, empty, error, mobile, and routine product states, not only a showcase screen?

Avoid numeric scoring unless the user has defined weights. A direction that photographs well but fails routine product states is not complete.

## Produce a direction brief

The brief should contain only decisions that help another designer or implementer preserve the idea:

- context and assumptions;
- chosen visual thesis;
- experience posture and content voice;
- focal path and layout principle;
- type, color, imagery, icon, surface, and motion direction;
- signature device and restraint rules;
- responsive and state implications;
- proposed tokens or system changes;
- examples of choices that fit and choices that would break the thesis;
- risks, validation needs, and open questions.

Use words, token proposals, simple diagrams, wireframes, or mockup specifications as appropriate. If generating an exploratory image or comp is available and requested, label it as a design artifact rather than production UI.

## Avoid monoculture

Do not equate high quality with a fixed collection of premium fonts, large whitespace, dark surfaces, glass effects, bento grids, editorial layouts, or elaborate motion. These can be appropriate outcomes, but they are not evidence of senior design by themselves.

Do not ban common patterns merely because agents overuse them. Ask whether the choice is purposeful, specific, and well executed. The goal is not novelty at every point; it is an interface whose familiar and distinctive elements both feel intentional.
